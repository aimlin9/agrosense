AgroSense’s diagnosis model is a 2.7 MB file. It recognises 38 classes — 26 diseases and 12 healthy states — across 14 crops, at 97.28% validation accuracy. Here’s how it was built, and why we don’t lean on that number as hard as it might seem.

Starting from MobileNetV2

Training a network from scratch needs far more data and compute than a startup has. Instead we used transfer learning: start from MobileNetV2, a network pretrained on millions of general images and designed to run efficiently on phones, and teach it the new task.

The training data was PlantVillage: 54,305 images of leaves across 38 classes, trained on a free Kaggle P100 GPU.

Phase one: train the head

We froze the pretrained base and trained only a new classification head for 10 epochs at a learning rate of 1e-3. The base already knows edges, textures and shapes; the head learns which combinations mean early blight or common rust. Validation accuracy reached 94.6%.

Phase two: fine-tune carefully

Next we unfroze the top 30 layers and trained for another 10 epochs with a learning rate 100 times smaller. Too high a rate here wipes out what the base learned. Validation accuracy climbed to 97.28%.

Small enough for the real world

Exported to TensorFlow Lite with default quantisation, the 10 MB Keras model became 2.7 MB with negligible accuracy loss. Today it runs on the server, which keeps the app light; the size means on-device diagnosis without internet is realistic next.

Why we don’t oversell 97%

PlantVillage photos are taken in controlled conditions: one leaf, plain background, even light. Photos from a farm are messier. A validation score on clean images is a ceiling, not a promise.

So the product is built around honesty rather than the headline number:

  • Crop-aware predictions. The farmer chooses the crop, and we restrict the answer to diseases of that crop. If the photo doesn’t look like that crop at all, confidence is capped and flagged.
  • Visible confidence. Every result shows how sure the model is; below 60% it asks for a better photo and an expert.
  • Experts in the loop. Extension officers can review and correct results, and those corrections become training data.

On two public-domain field photos of tomato late blight the model had never seen, it answered Late blight at 99.9% and 100%. Encouraging — and exactly why we now need hundreds of real photos from Ghanaian farms to measure properly.

What’s next

Field data from pilot partners, models for Ghana’s staples — cassava, cocoa, yam and plantain — and on-device inference. The code is open on GitHub.
