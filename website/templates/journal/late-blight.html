Late blight is one of the few crop diseases that can destroy a tomato field in under two weeks. The good news is that it gives warning signs. Catch them in the first days and you can usually save most of the crop.

Why it moves so fast

Late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould rather than a true fungus. In cool, wet conditions a single infected leaf can release huge numbers of spores, carried by wind and rain splash to the next plants within days. That is why timing matters more than anything else.

The best time to act on late blight is the first day you suspect it — not the day you’re sure.

The first signs

  • Grey-green, water-soaked patches on leaves, often at the tips or edges, that turn dark brown within a day or two.
  • White, fuzzy growth under the leaf in humid mornings, at the edge of the dark patches.
  • Dark, greasy streaks on stems.
  • Firm brown blotches on green fruit that spread across it.

Early blight looks similar but is slower: its spots have rings inside them, like a target, and start on the oldest leaves. AgroSense distinguishes the two and shows how sure it is.

When to watch most closely

Cool nights between 10 and 20°C, long periods when leaves stay wet, fog and steady rain. In Ghana that means the main rainy season, and the highland growing areas in particular. AgroSense’s weather screen flags wet weeks so you know when to inspect daily.

What to do on day one

  1. Pull out and destroy the worst-affected plants. Burn or bury them — don’t leave them in the field or compost them.
  2. Don’t work among plants while they are wet; you’ll carry spores on your hands and clothes.
  3. Protect the healthy plants. Copper-based sprays are accepted in organic farming; apply them before rain and repeat every five to seven days in wet weather.
  4. If it keeps spreading, ask your extension officer about a protectant like mancozeb or a systemic product combining metalaxyl and mancozeb — and rotate products to avoid resistance.
  5. Check neighbouring plants every day.

Preventing it next season

  • Use certified, disease-free seedlings.
  • Stake and prune plants so leaves dry quickly; water at the base in the morning.
  • Never plant tomato next to potato — they share the disease.
  • Destroy volunteer tomato and potato plants after harvest.
